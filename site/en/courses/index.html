Main course “Medical Latin” in 2 months

For students of medical, dental and pharmacy faculties in any year, future medical students and medical-lyceum pupils. You won’t just memorise the material: you’ll understand how the terminological language of medicine works.

Next group starts: 11 January 2027

Express course: 6 days before your final module test

For medical students whose final module test is close and for whom prescriptions, diagnoses and clinical terms still feel like chaos. It’s revision and structure, not learning from scratch.

Next groups: 8 February, 22 February and 9 March 2027

Sound familiar?

  • Prescriptions, diagnoses and clinical terms have merged into one stream of information.
  • You mix up what belongs to the noun and what to the adjective, and how to agree the endings.
  • Endless cramming without understanding the logic, and constant stress before the oral test.

Who it’s for

  • students before their final module test or exams;
  • those who have studied Latin and want a quick revision;
  • those who want to find their gaps and build a system.

Programme: 6 days, 6 lessons

Lessons 1–⁠2. Grammar and anatomical terminology

We put grammar in order: you stop mixing up noun declensions and adjective groups, and learn to use reference tables instead of blind cramming.

  • Nouns (declensions I–V). Non-agreed attributes: a clear algorithm for building them.
  • Adjectives (groups I and II). Agreed attributes in every case, singular and plural.
  • The different types of complex anatomical phrases, and the usual exam traps.

Lessons 3–⁠4. Prescriptions and pharmaceutical terminology

The structure of a prescription line, step by step: from the right case for the dosage form and the active substance to standard abbreviations and the signature.

  • Rules for writing and reading prescriptions: solid, soft and liquid dosage forms.
  • Chemical nomenclature: acids, oxides and salts.
  • Prescription abbreviations, the names of medicinal plants and drugs, standard phrases.

Lessons 5–⁠6. Clinical terminology and a mock final test

We learn to understand and build clinical terms from prefixes, roots and suffixes, practise the right stress, and sit a mock final module test.

  • Stress in clinical terms, basics of word formation and affixes.
  • Building terms from the most frequent Greek morphemes (-⁠tomia, -⁠stomia, -⁠ectomia, brachy-, brady-).
  • The 30 most frequent diagnoses (vitium cordis congenitum, cicatrices post variolam), your questions answered, and a mock final test.

Check yourself in 60 seconds

Three real tasks from the course: anatomy, prescriptions, clinical terms.

Anatomical terminology

How do you put facies anterior (anterior surface) into the genitive plural?

  • faciorum anteriorium
  • facierum anteriorum
  • facies anteriores
Answer

The answer is facierum anteriorum. Facies, ei f is a 5th-declension noun (Gen. pl. -⁠erum), and anterior, ius is a comparative adjective, declined like a 3rd-declension consonant stem (Gen. pl. -⁠um).

Prescriptions

What does M., f. linim. D.S. mean at the end of a prescription?

  • Take a liquid ointment in equal parts. Give.
  • Give such doses in tablets. Label.
  • Mix to make a liniment. Give. Label.
Answer

The answer is “Mix to make a liniment. Give. Label.” M., f. linim. = Misce, fiat linimentum; D.S. = Da. Signa.

Clinical terminology

How do the Greek prefixes brachy- and brady- differ?

  • Both mean “disorder”.
  • brachy- means “short”, brady- means “slow”.
  • brachy- means “slow”, brady- means “short”.
Answer

The answer is: brachy- = short (brachydactylia), brady- = slow (bradykinesia, slowness of movement).

Frequently asked questions

Can I really learn medical Latin in 6 days?

Yes, if it’s revision and structure rather than learning from scratch. In 6 days you’ll pull everything into one system and close the gaps.

Is it for me if I don’t understand anything right now?

Yes, that’s exactly who it’s for. We go through the basics in plain words, the most important topics and students’ typical mistakes. It doesn’t make things harder: it explains what was unclear.

Will it help me pass my module test or exam?

Yes, that’s the focus: the key topics that really come up, the logic of declensions and terms, and a structure so you don’t mix things up.

How much time a day does it take, and how long are the sessions?

On average 30–⁠60 minutes a day. Zoom sessions last 60–⁠100 minutes.

Who teaches the course, and why trust them?

A Latin teacher with 17 years at Bogomolets National Medical University and an examiner since 2011. So we’ll practise exactly what exams ask and where students most often go wrong.

Will it close my gaps?

Yes, that’s one of its main goals. You’ll find your weak spots, see where you’re unsure and close them systematically. But you’ll need to learn vocabulary on your own.

Will I be able to read and write prescriptions afterwards?

Yes, if you have basic vocabulary. You’ll understand how a prescription is built and stop fearing Latin in your studies.

How is it different from other courses?

It’s the essentials concentrated into 6 days, focused on students’ typical mistakes, and explained through logic rather than cramming. Nothing extra: only what helps you pass and understand.

“Confident Pronunciation” intensive: 3 days

You know the material but don’t sound sure? In 3 days you’ll learn to read terms without freezing, understand the logic of pronunciation instead of memorised rules, and put the stress in the right place.

Programme

  1. Start reading. Pronouncing every letter and letter combination, with practice from the first lesson.
  2. Stop mixing things up. Stress in Latin words.
  3. Sound like a professional. Professional vocabulary and how it is stressed and read: anatomical, pharmaceutical, chemical and clinical.

How it works

  • 90% of the time is practice: you read aloud constantly, hear yourself and others, and get your pronunciation corrected on the spot.
  • Everyone reads, no exceptions.
  • Mini-groups of 5–⁠10, with personal feedback on pronunciation.
  • Interactive exercises as homework.
  • For the 1–⁠2 most active participants: a free lesson on any problem topic, or 2–⁠3 interactive exercises on difficult vocabulary (up to 30 words), your choice.

Who it’s for

Students of medical, dental and pharmacy faculties, interns, pharmacists and anyone who works with Latin and wants to sound confident.
